Company Overview

What They Do

Aditya Infotech is India's largest Indian-owned video-surveillance company, selling under the CP PLUS brand. The distinction matters: the company's own commissioned Frost & Sullivan report names Prama Hikvision as the largest player in India, and Prama's FY25 India revenue of ₹3,377 Cr exceeded Aditya's ₹3,112 Cr, at a better PAT margin. Founded 1994 by Hari Shanker Khemka; CP PLUS launched 2007 by son Aditya Khemka, now MD. It makes IP, HDCVI and analog cameras, DVRs and NVRs, access control and edge-AI analytics, sold to homes, retail, enterprises, government and smart cities.

Distribution is the business's real asset: 1,012 distributors, 2,178 system integrators, 550+ cities, 41 branch offices, 13 RMA service centres and 141 exclusive “Galaxy” stores. Manufacturing is a single plant at Kadapa, Andhra Pradesh (4.1 lakh sq ft, ~2.5m units/month, ~80% utilisation), run through wholly-owned AIL Dixon. Every other facility — the housing plant, Noida Sector 68, the Orient Cables JV, Greater Noida — is still under construction.

Key Competitive Advantage: a 25-year pan-India distribution and service network in a market where the installer, not the end user, picks the brand. Expensive and slow to replicate — and the only one of the five moat dimensions the company actually built for itself.

Competitive Advantage (Moat) Analysis

DimensionRatingCommentary
Brand StrengthModerateCP PLUS is the largest Indian-owned surveillance brand and the spend is real — ₹92.2 Cr on advertising in FY25, 2.96% of revenue, across TV, radio, print and 141 exclusive Galaxy stores. But brand never converted into pricing power: EBITDA margin sat at 7–8% from FY23 through FY25, and CARE's own read is that “the lack of differentiation in the product offerings made the vendors adopt competitive pricing strategies.” On the RHP's own peer table, Prama Hikvision earned more PAT than Aditya on comparable revenue before the certification regime.
Network EffectsWeak / AbsentNone in the economic sense, and the disclosure supports that. System-integrator count has gone nowhere — 2,018 (FY23) → 2,208 (FY24) → 2,178 (FY25). The RHP states plainly that “we do not enter into long-term agreements with our distributors… our distributors are generally not obliged to continue purchasing products from us.” The same integrators carry Hikvision, Uniview and Dahua. A cloud app exists but no user count, MAU or subscription revenue is disclosed anywhere.
Switching CostsWeakCameras, NVRs, DVRs and PTZs are ~79% of revenue — interoperable, standards-based commodity hardware. There is no enterprise VMS with a defended installed base, and the RHP does not claim one. The customer base is a long tail, not sticky accounts: top customer 4.31%, top 10 just 18.80%. At end of life (5–7 years) rip-and-replace is routine and cheap — and the installer, not the end user, picks the brand.
Cost AdvantageModerateScale is genuine: Kadapa runs 17.2m units/year across 4.1 lakh sq ft on six SMT lines — real PCB assembly, not box-packing — and gross margin moved 16.9% → 18.3% → 21.5% (FY23–25). But the “Make in India” framing needs unpicking: AIL Dixon, the entity that actually manufactures, sourced only 15.25% of its materials from India in FY25 and imports ~85% from China in unassembled form. The parent's flattering “96.03% sourced from India” is a group-structure artefact — its largest supplier, at 51.92% of material cost, is its own subsidiary. The advantage is duty arbitrage on SKD assembly plus labour, not deep backward integration. Note what is being integrated: lenses, housings, cables. All mechanical — no sensors, no silicon.
Patents / Proprietary TechWeakThe prospectus settles this. Risk Factor 35: “Our designs and products are not patented.” It adds that know-how “may not be adequately protected… we may have no recourse against copies of our products and designs.” R&D was ₹17.6 Cr in FY25 — 0.57% of revenue, with 86 of 1,274 employees, and that figure includes capex. The company spends 5.2× more on advertising than on R&D. Camera design is contracted out to VVDN Technologies; SoCs are Ambarella (US) and Novatek (Taiwan). Inside their own prospectus, rival Sparsh CCTV is recorded as spending ~8% of revenue on R&D — fourteen times the rate.
What This Moat Does NOT Protect Against

Technology obsolescence — they do not own the technology. Margin compression as certification broadens beyond the current holders. Prama Hikvision, which has certified six models to CP PLUS's five, runs a Vasai plant and 21 RMA centres — it is inside the tent, not locked out. The migration of value to cloud and VSaaS software they do not own. And the loss or contraction of the Dahua agreement, which auto-renews in six-month increments and is terminable without cause.

Synthesis: The real moat is the pan-India distribution and service network — 1,012 distributors, 2,178 integrators, 550+ towns, 13 RMA centres, built over 25 years in a market where the installer chooses the brand. It is the only one of the five dimensions the company actually built. But it is not what is driving today's economics. What doubled margins in FY26 is a regulatory licence — the STQC regime effective 9 April 2025 — which the company's own commissioned industry report calls a “short-term advantage” that global players “possess vast R&D resources… to eventually adapt” to, after which “increased competition could lead to price wars.”

What Happened & Why It Matters
The defensible reading. Q1 FY27 was an outstanding operating quarter: revenue ₹1,402.4 Cr (+89.5% YoY), EBITDA ₹207.8 Cr at 14.8% (+604bp), adjusted PAT ₹142.2 Cr (+332%). Growing revenue ~50% in a distribution business with 121 debtor days and 134 inventory days genuinely consumes cash; roughly ₹2,000 Cr of incremental FY27 sales at those terms absorbs several hundred crore of working capital. Funding that with equity rather than debt, from a position of near-zero gearing, is conservative rather than reckless. Sector Market Size

India video-surveillance market. FY25 actual ▬; FY26E–FY30E projected ▬ at the 16.46% CAGR in the company's own commissioned Frost & Sullivan report, anchored to Motilal Oswal's ₹227bn FY30 estimate.
The structural case is real. India runs 1.1 cameras per 100 people against 14.4 in China and 15.3 in the USA. Under-penetration on that scale is not a narrative — it is arithmetic, and it is the strongest single argument for the equity.
Border surveillance is the demand pool to watch. Alongside the import restrictions on Chinese CCTV, there is a substantial build-out along the India–Bangladesh and India–Pakistan frontiers: the BSF has deployed 674 PTZ and static cameras across Berhampore, Malda and Jaisalmer, and a wider “Smart Border” programme pairs cameras with drones and radar into a single surveillance grid. This sits inside the common-infrastructure segment — 24.9% of the market and the fastest-growing at 18.6% CAGR. As one of only seven STQC-certified vendors, CP PLUS is positioned to bid. Note: no CP PLUS border contract is disclosed — the 674-camera BSF deployment was executed by Brihaspathi Technologies. Treat this as an addressable pool, not booked revenue.

The AIL Dixon Round Trip

In January 2024 a fire at a customs bonded warehouse destroyed ₹1,770 mn of AIL Dixon stock. On consolidation, management judged full recovery improbable and wrote its entire ₹294.5 mn JV carrying value to zero as an FY24 exceptional charge. Eight months later, on acquiring the remaining 50% for 7,305,805 shares issued to Dixon at ₹340.32, that same previously-held stake was fair-valued at ₹2,486.30 mn — a figure equal, to the rupee, to the share-swap consideration.

The acquired business had negative identifiable net assets of ₹(377) mn and posted an FY25 loss after tax of ₹112 Cr on negative net worth. The transaction therefore produced ₹535 Cr of goodwill — 52.6% of FY25 net worth — on a purchase price allocation the RHP expressly describes as provisional. None of this is improper, and the fire was real. But the valuation that set the swap ratio determined both the gain and the goodwill, in the financial year that became the IPO benchmark.

The thing that should make an investor pause: at ~95× trailing earnings and 24× book you are paying a technology-franchise multiple for a company whose own prospectus states in plain words that “our designs and products are not patented”, which spends 0.57% of revenue on R&D — a fifth of what it spends on advertising — whose FY26 margin doubling came not from anything it invented but from a certification rule its own commissioned report calls a short-term advantage, and which has converted ₹943 Cr of four-year reported profit into negative ₹84 Cr of operating cash.

Recommendation, Support & Resistance

Stage 1 · Anticipation
WATCH — DO NOT CHASE

The operating story is genuine and the technical structure is constructive, but three things sit unresolved this month: the ₹1,500 Cr QIP vote closes 19 September with the result by the 22nd; daily RSI is at 85 against the ₹3,940 resistance; and the stock trades under ASM Stage 1 on a 22.9% free float. Anticipation here means building a watchlist entry and a plan — not a position at the top of a mature fifth wave into a known supply event.

BUY ONLY ON EVIDENCE

Three confirmations, in order of importance:

1. Cash conversionH1 FY27 CFO turning materially positive
2. QIP resolvedPriced and absorbed without a de-rating
3. Price structureDecisive close above ₹3,940 on rising delivery

If H1 FY27 shows that inflecting, the multiple is arguably defensible on growth. If it does not, then 95× earnings is being paid for accounting profit, and the QIP will not be the last one.
